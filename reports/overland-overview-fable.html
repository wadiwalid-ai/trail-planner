The elevator pitch

Overland is a safety-first companion for driving where the map runs out.

Not a social trail app. A tool built for the moment cell signal disappears, the trail forks, or someone gets stuck — and for the years of driving before and after that moment.

Overland is a mobile app (iOS/Android/web, built on Expo/React Native) for people who drive off paved roads: desert overlanders in the UAE, and 4x4 / overland communities more broadly. It does four things well:

  1. Find and plan trails — a curated + community + OpenStreetMap trail library, browsable on an interactive offline-capable map, with an AI trip planner that turns "3 days, stock Land Cruiser, family-friendly" into a real itinerary. Every trail carries real technical data: distance, elevation gain, surface, difficulty — not marketing copy.
  2. Record and understand your own drives — GPS + telemetry recording that becomes a shareable community trail, with AI-generated trip summaries and auto-suggested waypoints (scenic, technical, summit) pulled from the actual sensor data.
  3. Track your group live, off-grid — "Convoy": live positions, speed, and status (moving / stopped / stuck / need-help) for everyone in your group, with recovery guidance when someone gets stuck.
  4. Stay findable when it matters — an SOS flow, offline Plus Codes, and a safety panel that works with zero signal, because the worst time to need a location fix is exactly when you have no bars.

02
Design philosophy

Every design choice traces back to one job: work when it's hot, bright, and you have one hand on the wheel.

Four themes, not one brand

Overland ships four complete visual identities — Dune, Overland, Apex, Horizon — each with its own palette, hero photography treatment, and typography weighting, all built on one shared component system (ThemeContext, persisted per-user).

DuneWarm ember / sand
OverlandDeep field green
ApexCharcoal / performance
HorizonGolden hour

Why: off-road culture is not one aesthetic — a Wrangler crawler club and a Land Cruiser desert convoy don't want the same app to look identical. Letting the community pick an identity costs nothing extra to build once the token system exists, and it turns the app into something people are proud to have on their home screen.

Editorial typography, not "startup UI"

Headlines and hero moments use a serif (Fraunces) against a clean sans body (Inter) — the same pairing used in this document. It reads more like a well-made outdoor magazine than a SaaS dashboard.

Why: the category is full of utilitarian, spreadsheet-like map apps. A magazine-grade visual bar signals "this was made by people who actually go off-road," which matters more in this niche than in most consumer categories — credibility is the entire sale.

Honesty over hype in every safety surface

The Safety Panel shows exact coordinates in two formats plus offline Plus Codes; the SOS button pre-fills a real SMS with real coordinates and queues it if there's no signal instead of pretending it sent. Offline map downloads show a real, calibrated size estimate and a hard ceiling (1.2GB / 45,000 tiles) rather than an optimistic guess.

Why: in a safety context, a UI that lies confidently is worse than one that says "I don't know." Every safety-adjacent screen is designed to fail loudly and honestly, never silently.

03
Where Claude fits

AI as a deterministic co-pilot, not a fortune teller.

Every text-generation feature in the app runs on Claude Sonnet 4.6 via Anthropic's API. We made one deliberate, non-obvious call early on that shapes all of it: reactive AI, never predictive.

What's built today

FeatureWhat Claude does
AI Trip PlannerTurns vehicle + duration + group + terrain preferences into a structured, day-by-day markdown itinerary.
Recovery co-pilot"TrailMaster" persona — a stuck-vehicle assistant that asks structured questions (surface, angle, gear, solo-or-group) and walks the driver through a recovery.
Track summariesReads a recorded drive's telemetry and writes a readable trip report with an assessed difficulty rating.
Waypoint suggestionsFlags likely scenic / technical / summit waypoints from altitude peaks and speed variance in the raw GPS log.
What we deliberately rejected Predictive hazard AI — "you'll likely lose a vehicle over that ridge." We tested this idea internally and killed it. Desert terrain shifts (dunes migrate metres per year) and most trails are unmapped at useful resolution — a wrong safety prediction is worse than no prediction at all, and erodes trust in every other feature.
What we kept instead Arithmetic on live GPS/mesh data — separation distance, no-movement duration, out-of-range alerts — is not "prediction," it's measurement. Fully deterministic, fully explainable, and it's the backbone of Convoy's status indicators.

We think this is the more interesting Claude story to tell: not "we bolted on AI everywhere," but "we drew a hard line about where language-model judgment belongs versus where it doesn't," and Claude sits precisely on the right side of that line — interpreting language and structuring guidance, never inventing facts about terrain it can't verify.

04
The honest answer

The app is not the moat. We designed it knowing that.

In the vibe-coding era, any single screen of this app could be rebuilt in a weekend. So the defensibility can't live in the code — it has to live in what the code accumulates over time.

1
The app / UIScreens, themes, navigation — genuinely cloneable fast with today's AI coding tools.
~1 weekend
2
Trail & feature logicOSM sync, recording pipeline, AI prompt design — takes real engineering time, but is still a solved-problem space.
~1–3 months
3
Deployed hardware + mesh densityA LoRa communicator in enough vehicles that Convoy actually works off-grid — this requires supply chain, a device business, and physical distribution.
~1–2 years
4
Compounding data + community trustEvery drive recorded by a real vehicle refines the terrain model and the recovery knowledge base a little more. A competitor starting today starts at zero — and can never buy their way to "years of real drives."
Compounds forever

Living 3D terrain — the clearest example

We start from GEDTM30, the best free 30m bare-earth global elevation model (CC BY 4.0, commercially usable). That's the same public data anyone can download. What no one else has: every recorded drive is a sensor pass — GPS + barometer for a relative elevation profile, phone IMU for pitch (grade) and roll (off-camber) — and enough passes over time refine a crowd-built terrain model no static dataset can match, especially over dune fields that migrate metres per year.

Sourcing rule: "buy the mountains, crowdsource the sand" — we'd license commercial high-resolution DEM only for stable terrain (mountains, wadis, rock) where it stays accurate for years; dune fields are refreshed by the community because paying to survey terrain the wind rewrites every season is money wasted.

The recovery knowledge base — the second example

Every real stuck-vehicle recovery, with what actually worked, by terrain type and vehicle — curated and weighted by verified experienced marshals — becomes a retrieval knowledge base Claude draws on. This is proprietary field data that cannot be scraped, licensed, or reproduced by a competitor without years of the same real-world usage.

We use retrieval over this knowledge base rather than fine-tuning a model on it — safer, auditable, and avoids hallucinating recovery advice, which in this context is a safety issue, not a quality issue.

The direct question

"Couldn't someone just clone this?"

Yes — the app. We're not pretending otherwise; that's why this whole brief is built around the layers beneath the app.

No — the fleet. Convoy's value is a function of how many vehicles around you are running the hardware. A clone launching today has zero deployed devices and zero network effect; density has to be earned, one vehicle and one club at a time.

No — the data. A crowd-refined terrain model and a verified recovery knowledge base are outputs of real usage over real time. There's no shortcut, no dataset to buy, no scrape target — the only way to get it is to have been running the product for years already.

No — the trust. Safety products live or die on community trust, especially with a marshal-verification layer gatekeeping data quality. That trust is a brand and relationship asset built through actual desert meetups and club relationships, not a feature flag.

No — the economics. A subscription-funded free hardware model (the WHOOP playbook) requires getting unit economics, churn, and device cost right simultaneously — a genuinely hard operating problem, not a design decision a clone can copy by looking at screenshots.

In one lineAnyone can copy what the app looks like in a weekend. Nobody can copy what it knows, who trusts it, or how many vehicles are already running it — without doing the same years of work we're doing right now.
07
Why this is worth Fable's time

An honest build, and a real relationship with the model underneath it.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 already sits at the center of the two features that matter most to users under stress — the trip planner people rely on before they lose signal, and the recovery co-pilot they open after something's gone wrong. We'd welcome the chance to go deeper: on-device or lower-latency options for the recovery flow when connectivity is marginal, retrieval patterns for the marshal-verified knowledge base as it grows, and multimodal handling for the photo/voice stuck-reports drivers send from the field.

Where we are Every feature described in this brief is live in the current build — not a deck of intentions. The hardware/mesh layer and the terrain data pipeline are the next builds, and where we'd most value Anthropic's counsel as we design the AI surface around them honestly, the same way we've tried to design everything else.
